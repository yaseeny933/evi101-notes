Causal Quasi-Experimental Evidence

Estimating a causal effect when you can't randomize. Big idea of the day, again: the counterfactual — compared to what?

as-good-as-random regression discontinuity statistical controls selection bias

01 The core idea

Causal evidence asks whether a change in a policy, procedure, or practice causes a change in an outcome. Session 5 answered this with the gold standard — the randomized controlled trial (RCT). But experiments aren't always possible, and today's question is: how do we build convincingly causal evidence without one?

Sometimes an experiment is unethical — you can't randomly assign some students to be suspended from school to see if suspension raises dropout rates. Sometimes it's simply not feasible — a state or national policy usually applies to everyone in a jurisdiction at once, so there are no implementers to compare against non-implementers, and you can't randomly assign states or nations to adopt it. In those cases we turn to quasi-experimental methods.

The counterfactual is the big idea again. To measure a causal impact we compare the treatment group's outcome to an estimate of the counterfactual — what would have happened to that same group if it had not been treated. Always ask, "compared to what?" The only thing that changes today is how the comparison group is created: instead of random assignment (RCT), a quasi-experiment finds a process that produces "as-good-as-random" assignment to treatment and comparison groups.

Same logic as an RCT — a valid counterfactual — but the groups are formed by an as-good-as-random process, not a coin flip.

There are many quasi-experimental methods — difference-in-differences, propensity score matching, instrumental variables regression, synthetic controls — and which one fits depends on the data you have. EVI101 focuses on just one, the workhorse that today's reading uses: the regression discontinuity design (RDD).

02 Regression discontinuity — a cut point does the randomizing

In education, students are constantly sorted into programs by an arbitrary cutoff: miss more than 15% of school days → mandatory summer school; earn above a 3.8 GPA → a college scholarship; score below a threshold → extra tutoring. That cutoff is exactly what makes a causal estimate possible.

Why you can't just compare treated to untreated

Take the summer-school rule. We can't simply compare students who attended summer school to those who didn't, because assignment wasn't random — and we have good reason to think the two groups differ. Kids absent often enough to qualify may have higher rates of illness, live far from school, or face other challenges, so they might do worse with or without the program. Comparing them to never-absent kids gives us no valid counterfactual: it's a selection-bias trap.

The RDD fix: zoom in on the students right at the cutoff. A student who missed 14% of days (just below, no summer school) and one who missed 16% (just above, assigned to summer school) are almost identical — both missed a lot of school — except that an arbitrary line put one in treatment and one in comparison. Near the cutoff, which side you land on is as good as random. So if test scores jump for the just-above group but not the just-below group, it's reasonable to attribute that jump to the treatment.

Regression discontinuity: the discontinuity (vertical jump) in the outcome exactly at the arbitrary cutoff — not the overall slope — is the estimated causal effect of the treatment.

The one big caveat: it's a local effect

An RDD can only tell us about the effect of treatment on students near the cutoff. From the summer-school example, we learn about kids who missed around 15% of days — not kids who missed only one or two days, or kids who missed half the year. Note also that researchers usually don't set the cutoff or design the policy; they work with the data they have and ask, "what method for building causal evidence best fits the data in front of me?"

Today's reading applies exactly this: Dee & Penner (2016)

Dee and Penner study the impact of encouraging ninth graders to enroll in a year-long ethnic studies course (focused on the experiences, perspectives, and histories of traditionally underrepresented racial and ethnic groups) on attendance, grades, and credits earned. Students whose 8th-grade GPA fell below 2.0 were encouraged to take the course; those above 2.0 were not. That clear cut point — GPA 2.0 — is what lets the authors run a regression discontinuity, just like the summer-school example but with GPA as the running variable.

03 Statistical controls & confounding — and why RDD still isn't an RCT

Even comparing students just above and below the 2.0 cutoff, we might worry that a difference in an outcome (attendance, credits) reflects a leftover difference in academic preparation rather than the course itself. The tool for this worry is statistical controls.

A confounding variable is a variable related to both the treatment and the outcome, which can masquerade as a treatment effect. In this study, 8th-grade attendance is a prime suspect: it's strongly correlated with 9th-grade attendance, GPA, and credits earned. Maybe the outcomes we see near the cutoff are really being driven by prior attendance, not by ethnic-studies eligibility.

To handle it, we add 8th-grade attendance to the statistical model as a control — mathematically, we hold that variable constant. We now compare outcomes between students who were and weren't eligible for ethnic studies among people with the same 8th-grade attendance, teasing out the prior-attendance relationship so the effect estimate reflects eligibility and nothing else. We aren't limited to one control — we can also hold constant gender, race, grade level, special-education and English-learner status, prior suspension, and more.

So why bother with RCTs at all? Because controls can only account for things we measured. If a factor that matters was never captured — and there always is one — we'll never know whether it, rather than the treatment, produced the difference. That is the fundamental limit of quasi-experiments: they defend against observed confounders, but unobserved confounders (omitted variables) remain a threat. Random assignment, by contrast, balances both measured and unmeasured factors — which is why it yields stronger internal validity.

04 Judging quasi-experimental causal evidence — three questions

As with every form of evidence, we judge causal evidence on its own terms. Three essential questions organize the appraisal — and Dee & Penner is a good stress test of each.

How well does the study describe the contrast between the treatment and the comparison group? (What is the treatment?)

The tricky part Not all encouraged students took the course, and some higher-GPA students did — so is the treatment being encouraged to enroll or actually participating? "Eligibility" better captures real-world impact (we usually can't force participation); "participation" better captures whether the course works for those who take it. Dee & Penner distinguish both. But RDD tells you whether there's an impact, not why or how to implement it — we'd want to know more about the course content and pedagogy.

How valid is the counterfactual? How well does the study deal with selection bias?

RDD's strength People who barely fall on either side of an arbitrary line should be alike except for treatment — so Dee & Penner can convincingly claim a strong counterfactual, at least for students near the 2.0 GPA cutoff. Statistical controls further guard against observed confounders like prior attendance.

How well do the authors identify the groups to which the treatment effect applies?

External validity Authors should report who was actually affected. Dee & Penner describe the institutional context and student demographics (pp. 135–136, Table 1) and repeatedly remind readers their estimates apply only to students near the 2.0 cutoff — a direct consequence of RDD's local nature.

Other quasi-experimental methods (named, not tested in EVI101): when there's no cutoff to exploit, researchers reach for other tools that manufacture an as-good-as-random comparison. A quick map:

⚠ Nuance & watch-outs

  • Quasi-experiments rest on an untestable assumption. The whole case depends on the comparison group being a valid counterfactual (near-cutoff similarity for RDD; parallel trends for DiD). You can argue for that assumption, but you can't prove it from the data.
  • Unobserved confounders are the Achilles' heel. Controls only neutralize variables you measured. Any omitted variable that drives both treatment and outcome can still bias the estimate — the reason RDD is not a substitute for an RCT.
  • Weaker internal validity than an RCT. Random assignment balances measured and unmeasured factors; a quasi-experiment only balances the ones it captured, so its causal claim is more conditional.
  • RDD estimates are local. They speak only to units near the cutoff — great internal validity there, limited external validity elsewhere. Don't extrapolate to people far from the threshold.
  • Describes impact, not mechanism. RDD shows whether a program worked, not why or how to replicate it — a limitation for practitioners wanting to implement.
  • Watch your causal language. A quasi-experiment can license causal claims, but only as strongly as its counterfactual. Match the strength of the wording to the strength of the design and be explicit about who the estimate applies to.

05 Today's application — the Brazilian ethnic-studies law

A 2003 Brazilian law requires that African and Afro-Brazilian history and culture be taught in history, art, and literature courses at the elementary and high-school levels. The in-class task uses the Dee & Penner evidence to argue about a requirement like this. As always, there's no single correct answer — the point is to use the language of evidence to argue for one choice over the other.

Best support? Long-term impact vs. replication

Option A: students encouraged to enroll were 15.7 pp more likely to graduate high school (a statistically significant follow-up result). Option B: results replicate across three of five piloting high schools. One speaks to magnitude & durability; the other to external validity across contexts.

Bigger limitation? What transfers to Brazil?

Option A: Dee & Penner report little on how the program works, so a leader couldn't easily adapt and implement it (RDD shows whether, not how). Option B: they don't report standardized test-score outcomes, so we don't know the effect on that kind of learning.

How to argue it in the language of evidence: ground every claim in Session 6 vocabulary. To support the requirement, a long-term graduation effect leans on magnitude and statistical significance, while the multi-school replication leans on external validity — deciding which matters more depends on the decision at hand. On limitations, note that regression discontinuity is designed to establish whether an impact exists near the cutoff, not to explain mechanism or generalize far from it — so the "we don't know how it works, so we can't implement it" critique cuts to the heart of applying this evidence in a new national context. A strong answer names the counterfactual, the population the estimate applies to, and the internal-vs-external-validity trade-off.

06 Glossary — quiz yourself first

Try to define each term before you reveal it. These are the Session 6 evidence-vocabulary words (plus the supporting concepts from the video).

counterfactual
What would have happened to the treatment group if it had not received the treatment. Causal evidence works by comparing the treatment group's actual outcome to an estimate of this counterfactual. The big idea of the day: "compared to what?" A valid comparison group is a stand-in for the treatment group's counterfactual.
as-good-as-random assignment
A process — not a literal coin flip — that sorts units into treatment and comparison groups in a way that is effectively random with respect to the outcome, so the groups are comparable. It's what lets a quasi-experiment stand in for an experiment. Near a cutoff, landing just above vs. just below is as good as random.
quasi-experiment
A study that produces convincingly causal evidence without random assignment, by exploiting a process that yields as-good-as-random treatment and comparison groups. Examples: regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences, matching, instrumental variables, synthetic controls. Used when an RCT is unethical or infeasible.
regression discontinuity (RDD)
A quasi-experimental design in which an arbitrary cutoff on a running variable determines who is eligible for a treatment. Units just above vs. just below the cutoff are nearly identical, so a jump (discontinuity) in the outcome exactly at the cutoff estimates the causal effect — for units near the cutoff. GPA below 2.0 → encouraged to take ethnic studies; GPA above → not.
confounding variable
A variable related to both the treatment and the outcome that can create a spurious apparent effect. Addressed by adding it to the model as a statistical control — if it was measured. 8th-grade attendance is correlated with both eligibility and 9th-grade outcomes.
statistical control
Adding a variable to a regression to hold it constant, so the estimated treatment effect reflects the treatment and not that other variable. Multiple controls can be included, but you can only control for factors you actually measured. Comparing eligible vs. ineligible students among those with the same prior attendance.
internal validity (for quasi-experiments)
How convincingly the study establishes that the treatment — not something else — caused the outcome, i.e., how valid the estimated counterfactual is. RDD has strong internal validity near the cutoff; quasi-experiments in general have weaker internal validity than RCTs because unmeasured confounders remain a threat. Near-cutoff comparability + controls = a strong (but not airtight) counterfactual.
external validity (for quasi-experiments)
How well the study's causal estimate generalizes to other people, places, or contexts. RDD estimates are local — they apply to units near the cutoff — so external validity is inherently limited and authors should specify exactly whom the effect applies to. Dee & Penner's effect applies to students near the 2.0 GPA cutoff, not all students.
selection bias
Systematic differences between treated and untreated groups that arise when assignment is not random, so a naïve comparison confounds the treatment effect with those pre-existing differences. Producing a valid counterfactual is precisely the effort to defeat it. Frequently-absent kids differ from never-absent kids for reasons unrelated to summer school.
causal language
Wording that asserts one thing causes another ("raised," "increased," "led to"). It should be used only as strongly as the design's counterfactual supports, and paired with a clear statement of the population to which the effect applies. "Encouragement raised attendance by ~6 days, for students near the cutoff."

07 iRAT prep — test yourself

Answer before revealing. Three are written like TBL readiness-check multiple-choice; the rest are short-answer/application. Then chew on the discussion prompts.

MC1. A district assigns every student who scores below 70 on a placement test to a mandatory tutoring program. To estimate the program's causal effect with a regression discontinuity, you should compare —
  • A. All tutored students to all non-tutored students
  • B. Students who scored just below 70 to students who scored just above 70
  • C. The highest scorers to the lowest scorers
  • D. Tutored students before vs. after the program
B. RDD's leverage is near the cutoff. Students just below vs. just above 70 are nearly identical except for treatment assignment, so a jump in outcomes at the cutoff estimates the causal effect. Comparing all tutored to all non-tutored (A) reintroduces selection bias, since the groups differ systematically in ability.
MC2. In the Dee & Penner study, why is a regression discontinuity possible at all?
  • A. Students were randomly assigned to ethnic studies by lottery
  • B. The authors ran a controlled experiment in a lab
  • C. An arbitrary GPA cutoff (2.0) determined who was encouraged to enroll
  • D. Every student in the district took the course
C. A clear cut point — 8th-grade GPA of 2.0 — decided who was encouraged to take the course. That arbitrary threshold makes students just above and just below comparable, which is exactly what an RDD exploits. There was no random lottery (A) and no experiment (B).
MC3. A researcher adds 8th-grade attendance, gender, and prior suspension as statistical controls and says, "Now this is just as good as an RCT." The best correction is —
  • A. Correct — controls make any design equivalent to an experiment
  • B. Wrong — controls make the estimate worse, not better
  • C. Wrong — controls only account for variables you measured; unobserved confounders can still bias the estimate
  • D. Wrong — you should never use control variables
C. Controls neutralize observed confounders, but there are always factors we didn't or couldn't measure. Random assignment balances measured and unmeasured factors — which is why a quasi-experiment with controls is still not a substitute for an RCT.
SA4. RDD gave Dee & Penner a strong counterfactual. Why can't they claim their effect applies to all ninth graders?
Because RDD identifies a local effect — it's only credible for students near the cutoff (here, GPA around 2.0). Students far above or below the threshold aren't comparable to anyone across the line, so the design says nothing causal about them. This is the internal-validity (strong, near the cutoff) vs. external-validity (limited) trade-off, and it's why the authors keep reminding readers who the estimate applies to.
SA5. Name the untestable assumption a regression discontinuity relies on, and contrast it with the key assumption behind difference-in-differences.
RDD: units just above and just below the cutoff are alike in all respects except treatment (continuity at the cutoff) — so any jump is the treatment's doing. Difference-in-differences: the parallel-trends assumption — absent treatment, the treated and comparison groups would have followed parallel paths over time. Both are assumptions about the comparison group serving as a valid counterfactual, and neither can be fully proven from the data — which is the core vulnerability of quasi-experiments.
Discussion 1We can't randomly assign students to be suspended to test whether suspension causes dropout. Sketch a quasi-experimental design (a plausible cutoff or as-good-as-random process) that could get at this causal question — and name the assumption your design would hinge on.
Discussion 2Most ethnic-studies research generates qualitative evidence in a critical/participatory paradigm, while Dee & Penner generate quantitative evidence in a postpositivist paradigm. What does the RDD reveal well, what does it miss, and how might your own lens on ethnic studies shape whether you find it convincing?

★ Remember this

  • The counterfactual is the whole game — again. Causal evidence compares the treatment group to an estimate of what would have happened without treatment. Always ask "compared to what?"
  • Quasi-experiments swap random assignment for as-good-as-random assignment — the way to get causal evidence when an RCT is unethical or infeasible.
  • Regression discontinuity uses an arbitrary cutoff: units just above vs. just below are comparable, so a jump in the outcome at the cutoff is the causal effect — but only near the cutoff.
  • Statistical controls handle observed confounders by holding them constant — yet unmeasured confounders remain, which is why RDD is not a substitute for an RCT.
  • Appraise with three questions: How clear is the treatment–comparison contrast? How valid is the counterfactual (selection bias)? Whom does the effect apply to?
  • Match your causal language to your design. Strong counterfactual → confident, but specify the local population and stay honest about weaker internal validity than an experiment.
