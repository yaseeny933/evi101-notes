01 The core idea & where it picks up

Session 3 introduced Calarco & Small's five quality factors for judging descriptive qualitative evidence — cognitive empathy, heterogeneity, palpability, follow-up, and self-awareness. Today we go deeper on the two that describe the relationship between researcher and participant: cognitive empathy and self-awareness.

Cognitive empathy is the big idea of the day. Studies that exhibit high cognitive empathy help us understand individual participants as they understand themselves — so we grasp their perceptions, motivations, and beliefs. In other words, they let us step into the participants' shoes and see the world as they see it.

Cognitive empathy doesn't stop at reporting words and actions — it captures the meaning that produced them.

Session 3 gave us the logic of qualitative work (case logic, purposeful sampling, exposure over size). Session 4 explains why qualitative researchers deliberately get close to their participants — and why that closeness, handled well, is a source of trust rather than bias.

02 Cognitive empathy — beneath the words

Calarco & Small (Chapter 1) argue that cognitive empathy shows up not just by reporting what participants say or do, but by seeking to capture the perceptions, motivations, and beliefs that might influence those statements or actions.

Maria (Ch. 1, pp. 29–31): one line alone might mischaracterize her or invite judgment; the deeper the researcher digs, the more we understand her on her own terms.

Watch for the Maria example (pp. 29–31) when you read: at first we hear a single statement about her city and college applications that, taken alone, could be misread. By the third example the authors provide, we understand far more deeply why she feels as she does and how the researcher engaged her to reach that understanding. Developing cognitive empathy, Small & Calarco note, is a key component of high-quality qualitative evidence.

03 Two stances toward the researcher–participant relationship

Cognitive empathy exposes a deep difference in how the paradigms treat the gap between researcher and participant. It's not that one is careful and the other sloppy — they are pursuing trust by opposite routes.

Quantitative stanceQualitative stance
Goal in the relationshipObjectivity & detachmentCognitive empathy & closeness
Contact with participantsMinimize it — distance protects validityIncrease it — closeness deepens understanding
How trust is builtDetaching the researcher from the dataRich exposure to context & participants
Typical questionNot usually "how do participants see the world?"Centered on understanding the world as participants do

Qualitative researchers view closeness to participants and the research site as a way to increase the trust and quality of their findings — because it raises their exposure to context and their ability to let the audience step into participants' shoes. The quantitative worry ("you're too close, that's bias") is, in the qualitative paradigm, often exactly backwards.

04 Self-awareness & positionality

The flip side of getting close is being honest about it. Self-awareness is the extent to which the researcher understands and explains the influence of their own presence and assumptions. Because descriptive qualitative evidence is collected through interactions and relationships, the researcher must reckon with how they helped create that evidence.

A consideration of self-awareness does not make qualitative evidence weaker, more biased, or less rigorous. The way to increase trust and validity is to embrace and discuss subjectivity rather than try to avoid it. That is why you'll often see positionality statements in descriptive qualitative evidence.
What positionality namesEach person's identity is shaped by race, gender, class, sexuality, ethnicity, ability, and other social characteristics.
Why it mattersThese shape a researcher's perspective and biases — how they interact with participants, interpret evidence, and even which questions they ask and data they gather.
The aim of stating itNaming the identity elements that may have influenced the work is meant to improve the quality of the evidence and the research process.

Increasingly, positionality statements appear in quantitative research too. This is consistent with post-positivism: although there is an objective truth, we can only know it imperfectly and in part, because every researcher has a unique positionality that influences their work. Positionality is thus one dimension of the limitations we must weigh for any study.

When evaluating any study, always ask: How well supported are the claims by the type of evidence it represents, and what are the limitations of this evidence? Claims about people's beliefs, behaviors, motivations, and interpretations are more likely to be well supported by descriptive qualitative evidence than by descriptive quantitative evidence. The recurring quality question — applied to qualitative work

Ask the right people

Even the right type of evidence fails if you ask the wrong people. To make a claim about students' beliefs about whether teachers are helpful, interview students — not just teachers about their own practices. And qualitative interview data will usually give far more nuance and context about those beliefs than a simple 1-to-5 survey item ever could.

⚑ Nuance & watch-outs

  • Closeness is not automatically bias. In the qualitative paradigm, closeness to participants is often how researchers earn trust in their findings — the opposite of a defect.
  • Self-awareness ≠ weakness. A positionality statement doesn't confess that a study is flawed; it strengthens it by making the researcher's influence explicit and inspectable.
  • Judge a study by its own paradigm. Evaluating qualitative work by quantitative standards (or vice versa) is a category error — attend to the paradigm and its epistemology so you judge the evidence by its own standards.
  • Positionality is showing up in quant, too. Post-positivism accepts an objective truth we can only know imperfectly — so stating one's positionality is not a "soft science" quirk.
  • Relevance is judgment, not a checkbox. A study is almost never a perfect fit on every metric, and different context does not automatically make a study irrelevant — you need a real reason the difference would matter.

05 Relevance — from "there & then" to "here & now"

To recommend new evidence for São Paulo's Grand Challenge, you have to judge how relevant an existing study is to your context. Two guiding questions structure that judgment:

What inferences can you make from this study that would inform or apply to São Paulo's Grand Challenge?

topic fit Is the research about the problem you're trying to solve? A study on teaching reading to struggling readers in grades 6–9 is directly relevant to a challenge about grades 6–9 reading; one on beginning readers or social studies is less so.

How related are the setting and sample to São Paulo's context, priorities, and needs — and how or why would any differences matter?

there → here Look for shared characteristics beyond demographics: historical, social, legal, political, and cultural context all count (e.g., Brazil's federal requirement to teach Afro-Brazilian history).

Different context does not automatically make a study irrelevant. You can't dismiss a study just because it was done somewhere else — there needs to be a good reason why the difference would matter for the inference you want to make. Calarco's study was set in the U.S., but classrooms everywhere struggle to give all students equitable access to teacher support, so many of her help-seeking claims can travel.

There's no single indicator that settles relevance — it's a matter of judgment, and it's almost never a perfect fit. Carefully answering the two questions guides that judgment about how well any piece of research applies to your context, priorities, and needs.

✎ Today's application

To increase relevance beyond Calarco's U.S. setting, São Paulo's leaders want new research on how teachers and students from different socioeconomic classes interact in Brazilian schools. There's no correct answer — the task is to use the language of evidence (cognitive empathy, heterogeneity, palpability, follow-up, self-awareness, case logic, exposure) to argue for one path over the other. Both are enriching; funding allows only one. Both purposefully sample schools across socioeconomic backgrounds.

Option 1 observe · deep

6 months · 4 schools

Observation only, one full school day per week per school, across multiple grade levels. Detailed field notes on classroom set-up, materials, teachers' pedagogies, and student engagement.

  • Strong exposure — sustained time enables follow-up and rich context
  • Sees what actually happens in the room, not just self-report
  • But no interviews → weaker on cognitive empathy (the "why" behind actions)

Option 2 interview · voices

2 months · 20 schools

Interviews teachers twice (second interview designed from the first, digging deeper) and students once, about their perceptions of how teachers interact with them.

  • High cognitive empathy — reaches perceptions, motivations, beliefs
  • Follow-up built in — the second interview adapts to the first
  • Asks students directly about their own beliefs (right people)
  • But shorter, thinner exposure; relies on report, not observation

A worked line of argument — recommending Option 2 when the question is about how students experience class-based interactions:

Cognitive empathy — because the Grand Challenge cares about beliefs and perceptions, interviews reach the motivations behind behavior, exactly what the Maria example shows observation alone can miss.
Follow-up & the right people — the second interview, designed from the first, lets researchers dig deeper on specific practices; interviewing students directly means claims about students' perceptions come from students themselves.

↳ Or argue Option 1 on exposure and palpability: six months of firsthand observation captures what people do (not just say), avoids self-report bias, and supplies the concrete, contextual scenes that make findings palpable. Either way, name the factors and tie the affordances and limitations to the claim — a jump-start for what São Paulo should collect in Part 4 of the Grand Challenge.

Q1A study reports not only what a participant says, but the perceptions, motivations, and beliefs behind it, so we understand her as she understands herself. This best illustrates…
  • A. Heterogeneity
  • B. Cognitive empathy
  • C. Follow-up
  • D. Generalizability
B — Cognitive empathy. The big idea of the day: reaching beneath words and actions to the meaning that produced them, so we can step into the participant's shoes.
Q2Why do qualitative researchers often seek closeness to participants and the research site?
  • A. To reduce their exposure to context
  • B. Because closeness increases trust and quality by deepening exposure and understanding
  • C. To maximize objectivity and detachment
  • D. Because it guarantees the sample is representative
B. In the qualitative paradigm, closeness raises exposure and lets the audience step into participants' shoes — the reverse of the quantitative goal of detachment (A, C) and unrelated to representativeness (D).
Q3A researcher includes a statement describing how her race, class, and gender may have shaped her questions and interpretations. In the paradigm, this positionality statement is best understood as…
  • A. An admission that the study is biased and weak
  • B. A way to increase trust by embracing and explaining subjectivity
  • C. Proof the study lacks rigor
  • D. A substitute for collecting evidence
B — Self-awareness. Because qualitative evidence is created through relationships, explaining the researcher's influence strengthens the work; it does not make it weaker, more biased, or less rigorous.
Q4You want to claim that students find their teachers helpful. Which evidence is most convincing?
  • A. Interviews asking teachers about their helping practices
  • B. A district budget report on staffing
  • C. Interviews asking students directly whether and how their teachers help
  • D. A 1-to-5 survey item averaged across the school
C. The claim is about students' beliefs, so ask students — the right people. Rich interview data also gives more nuance and context than a simple survey scale (D) or teacher self-report (A).
Q5A study was conducted in the U.S., while São Paulo is in Brazil. The best way to judge its relevance is to…
  • A. Declare it irrelevant because the context differs
  • B. Assume it fully applies because education is universal
  • C. Ask what inferences apply and whether/why the setting-and-sample differences would matter
  • D. Check whether its sample was randomly drawn
C. Relevance is a judgment guided by the two questions. Different context alone doesn't make a study irrelevant (A) or relevant (B) — you need a good reason a difference would matter for your inference. Randomness (D) isn't the relevance test.

§ Glossary — quiz yourself first

Try to define each term before you reveal it.

cognitive empathy
Understanding individual participants as they understand themselves — capturing their perceptions, motivations, and beliefs, not just reporting what they say or do. It lets the audience step into participants' shoes and see the world as they see it. The big idea of the day and one of the five quality factors. The Maria example (pp. 29–31): by the third exchange we understand why she feels the way she does about her city.
self-awareness
The extent to which a researcher understands and explains the influence of their own presence and assumptions on the study. Because qualitative evidence is created through interactions and relationships, reflecting on this influence strengthens the work rather than weakening it — the route to trust is to embrace and discuss subjectivity, not avoid it. Often expressed through a positionality statement.
positionality
The idea that each person's identity is shaped by factors such as race, gender, class, sexuality, ethnicity, ability, and other social characteristics — which influence a researcher's perspective, experiences, and biases, affecting how they interact with participants, interpret evidence, and even which questions they ask and data they gather. A "positionality statement" names the identity elements a researcher thinks may have influenced their research, aiming to improve its quality.
paradigms
The underlying frameworks of assumptions (about reality, knowledge, and how to build trust) that a body of research operates within — e.g., the quantitative paradigm's pursuit of detachment vs. the qualitative paradigm's pursuit of closeness. Evaluating quality requires attention to the paradigm so you judge a study by its own standards. Judging qualitative work by quantitative criteria (or vice versa) is a paradigm error.
epistemology
A theory of knowledge — what counts as knowing, and how we can come to know it. Different paradigms rest on different epistemologies, which is why they seek trust in different ways. Post-positivism is one such view: there is an objective truth, but we can only know it imperfectly and in part, because every researcher has a unique positionality that influences their work. Post-positivism is why positionality statements increasingly appear even in quantitative research.
relevance
How well a study applies to your own context, priorities, and needs — a matter of judgment, not a checklist. Guided by two questions: (1) what inferences from the study would inform your challenge, and (2) how related are its setting and sample, and how or why would any differences matter? Different context alone does not make a study irrelevant. Moving evidence from "there and then" to "here and now" — e.g., asking whether Calarco's U.S. help-seeking findings apply to São Paulo.
