EVI101 · Session 7

Process Evidence

The evidence that opens the black box: not just whether something worked, but how and why it worked — and in what context.

context mechanisms triangulation levers of change

01 The core idea

Causal evidence answers "Does it work?" Process evidence answers the harder pair of questions that comes next: "How and why does it work?" It tells us about the causal processes and mechanisms behind an impact — as distinct from the causal impact itself.

Our two big ideas for the day are context and mechanisms. Context is the environment in which an intervention or process happens — legal, historical, social, political, community. Mechanisms are the features of an intervention that actually produce the impacts (or the lack of impacts) we observe. Understanding both is how we find the levers of change inside educational systems — which is why process evidence is so central to bridging research and practice.

Recall the Dee & Penner study on ethnic studies from earlier in the course. Causal evidence told a Boston superintendent that the course improved attendance, grades, and credits earned. But it couldn't say how: Was it the teachers' enthusiasm? A more engaging curriculum that pulled students into attending? An effect on students' social identity development? Something about San Francisco's diverse setting? We knew the course worked; we didn't know the mechanism or the contextual ingredients. That gap is exactly what process evidence fills.

Causal evidence measures treatment → outcome. Process evidence opens the box in the middle — the mechanisms — and situates the whole thing inside its context.

Process vs. descriptive vs. causal — three quick contrasts

Two questions come up constantly. First, how is process evidence different from descriptive evidence? Descriptive evidence (qualitative or quantitative) tells us what is happening; process evidence looks deeper at the social forces that produce those results — the how and why. Second, if it explains causes, is it "causal research"? Not in the sense we use in this course. "Causal" here means experimental or quasi-experimental (counterfactual) evidence. Process evidence is a different route to cause and effect — focused on the mechanisms that create change over time, in specific contexts.

Question it answersForm of evidenceWhat it delivers
What is happening?Descriptive (qual / quant)A faithful account of conditions or patterns — no causes.

How & why does it work?ProcessThe mechanisms and contextual features producing the impact.
Why "why" questions resist experiments: consider "Why do nations invest in education?" You can't randomize some nations to have contributing factors and others not. As Nobel laureate Angus Deaton notes, historians study long-term causal processes — no single event explains the outcome; instead they trace the causal mechanisms unfolding over decades in specific national contexts. Answering how/why questions usually needs multiple sources of evidence across the whole evidence framework — different data types, disciplines, even paradigms. That is what makes process evidence distinctive.

02 Mechanisms in action — the Oliveira study

Today's reading (Oliveira et al., 2024) is anthropological process evidence: it examines Venezuelan refugee elementary students in Northern Brazil and asks how teachers and students engage and learn from one another to build welcoming, caring spaces — and how harmful stereotypes get reinforced.

Compare it to the Calarco article from earlier: both care about children's agency and teacher–student engagement. But Calarco asks what children and teachers do (and how that maps to socioeconomic status) — descriptive. Oliveira et al. ask how engagement occurs and how classroom culture develops — process. Same classroom scene, different question.

To identify mechanisms, the authors triangulate a rich, diverse set of data: classroom observations, interviews with teachers, caregivers, and administrators, and key documents (curricula, policies, grading), plus students' drawings and artifacts. From this they name two mechanisms for the process they study — and, cleverly, they present one from the teachers' perspective and one from the children's:

Portuguese-only teaching teachers' view

Teachers enforce Portuguese as an act of care and compliance — helping immigrant students adapt to their new country. The same mechanism that welcomes also suppresses Spanish in the school environment.

Mechanism: a single practice does two things at once — builds belonging and reinforces a linguistic hierarchy.

Children's solidarity work children's view

Students push back against the rigid Portuguese-only approach — holding parallel conversations in Spanish during play and academic work, sustaining their language and identity from below.

Mechanism: children exercise agency, co-constructing a classroom culture the official rules don't capture.

↳ Weaving both perspectives together builds cognitive empathy and increases trust in the results. Teachers alone would show us Portuguese-as-care but not how children respond; children alone would show resistance but not the teachers' motivations. Together they give a robust picture — and, crucially, the reader is invited to ask whether these are the most plausible mechanisms, or whether some alternative explanation fits better.

03 Judging quality — the three process questions

Evaluating process evidence happens in two layers. First, identify what type(s) of data it draws on and apply that form's quality questions. The Oliveira piece is qualitative, so you'd apply the descriptive-qualitative questions (case logic in sampling, cognitive empathy, describing context). Had it used quantitative, causal, or synthetic data, you'd layer in those questions too.

Then, on top of that, ask the three questions unique to process evidence:

How well does the study identify the mechanisms by which an outcome is produced — including by ruling out plausible alternatives?

Mechanism Oliveira et al. use rich, triangulated data (observations, interviews, documents, artifacts) to name two mechanisms. The reader's job: are these convincing, or is there a more plausible alternative explanation?

How well does the study describe how relevant features of the local context might affect this process?

Context The authors devote early pages to the political, historical, and social context, and describe their methods. A gap they note: little on the two particular schools — their histories, unique pedagogies, or how teachers came to work there.

How well does the study explore how participants interpret and respond to this process?

Interpretation By splitting findings into teachers' and children's perspectives, the study captures how each group makes meaning of the same practice — a fuller, more comprehensive account of the two classrooms.

↳ And always ask about relevance to your own challenge: how related are the setting and sample to your context, priorities, and needs — and would any differences matter? Because this study is qualitative, judge relevance by authenticity (would the analysis ring true to teachers and students in São Paulo?), not by statistical generalizability the way you would a quantitative piece.

04 Nuance & watch-outs

Process evidence is powerful precisely because it goes where causal evidence can't — but that power comes with traps. Keep these in view.

⚠ Nuance & watch-outs

  • Process explains; it doesn't by itself prove impact. Naming a plausible mechanism is not the same as demonstrating a treatment caused an outcome. Process evidence pairs with causal evidence — the causal study says "it worked," the process study says "here's why."
  • A mechanism is a claim, not a fact. The bar is ruling out plausible alternatives. If a different explanation fits the same data as well, the mechanism isn't established. Stay skeptical of the author's preferred story.
  • The same mechanism can cut both ways. Portuguese-only teaching is care and suppression at once. Mechanisms aren't automatically "good levers" — they can produce inequity alongside inclusion.
  • Context is not decoration — it's a condition. A mechanism that works in one setting may misfire in another. Weak description of local context (e.g., the specific schools) limits how much you can transfer the finding.
  • Implementation matters. An observed impact bundles the program's elements and how well they were implemented with the surrounding environment. Fidelity of implementation is part of the mechanism story — a good idea poorly implemented can look like a failed idea.
  • Don't over-generalize from qualitative process work. Judge it on authenticity and depth, not on whether it "represents" a population. A vivid two-classroom study earns trust by ringing true, not by sample size.

05 Glossary — quiz yourself first

Try to define each term before you reveal it. These are the Session 7 evidence-vocabulary words.

context
The environment in which an intervention or process is happening — its legal, historical, social, political, and community surroundings. Some contextual features enable a program's success; others pose challenges. Describing context well is one of the three quality tests for process evidence. Oliveira et al. open by situating their study in the political and social history of Venezuelan migration to Northern Brazil.
mechanism
The feature(s) of an intervention that actually contribute to producing the impacts — or lack of impacts — we observe. Mechanisms are the "how and why" between a treatment and its outcome. Good process evidence identifies them and rules out plausible alternatives. "Portuguese-only teaching as an act of care" is a proposed mechanism for how teachers build belonging while suppressing Spanish.
triangulation
Using multiple, diverse sources or types of data to converge on and corroborate a finding — strengthening confidence in an identified mechanism. Process questions often require evidence from across the whole evidence framework. The study combines classroom observations, interviews (teachers, caregivers, administrators), curricular documents, and student drawings.
plausible alternatives
Rival explanations for the same observed process or outcome. Identifying a mechanism convincingly means showing it fits the evidence better than the competing accounts — not just that it's consistent with the data. As you read, ask: is "children's solidarity work" really the driver, or could another explanation account for the Spanish side-conversations?
cognitive empathy
The extent to which a researcher comes to understand how individuals view themselves and their own situations. Central to descriptive-qualitative quality — and deepened here by presenting both teachers' and children's perspectives. Weaving both viewpoints together increases our trust that the authors grasp what each group actually experiences.
levers of change
The features inside an educational system that, if adjusted, can produce more learning and more equity in opportunity and outcomes. Process evidence is prized by practitioners because it locates these levers — bridging research and practice. Knowing why ethnic studies improved outcomes tells a superintendent which ingredient to invest in and scale.

06 Today's application — coaching teachers in São Paulo

São Paulo leaders are weighing whether to coach teachers to sustain students' native languages and identities in multilingual classrooms with immigrant and Brazilian students. The task: which claim from the Oliveira article best supports this decision, and why? There's no correct answer — the point is to use the language of evidence to argue for one choice over the other.

Claim 1 · p. 547 Care & compliance

"Teachers' approach to children's Portuguese learning is an act of both care and compliance." Frames the coaching decision around the teacher-side mechanism: reshape how caring intentions are enacted so they don't suppress home languages.

Claim 2 · p. 548 Children's own boundaries

"The boundaries observed by teachers… regarding the hyperfocus and need to use and learn Portuguese were not how Brazilian and Venezuelan children spent time with one another." Frames coaching around the children's-solidarity mechanism: build on the multilingual culture kids already create.

Defend your pick using the ladder of inference sentence frames — climbing deliberately from data to action:

Select data"The researchers found that…" — name the specific observation or claim you're standing on.
Interpret"This shows that…" — say what mechanism the data reveals.
Conclude"This matters because…" — connect the mechanism to the coaching decision.
Act"So we should…" — the design choice for the coaching program.
The deeper design question: which processes or mechanisms from the article should a São Paulo coaching program replicate, and which should it avoid? A strong answer treats the two mechanisms as levers — e.g., replicate and amplify children's solidarity work; redesign the "care" mechanism so belonging no longer requires suppressing Spanish — and checks relevance by authenticity (would this ring true in São Paulo's classrooms?) rather than by generalizability.

07 iRAT prep — test yourself

Answer before revealing. Two are written like TBL readiness-check multiple-choice; the rest are short-answer/application. Then chew on the discussion prompts.

MC1. A randomized trial shows a tutoring program raised test scores. Leaders want to know why it worked so they can scale it. Which form of evidence best answers that?
  • A. More causal (experimental) evidence — run the trial again
  • B. Descriptive quantitative evidence — report the average gain
  • C. Process evidence — identify the mechanisms and contextual features producing the impact
  • D. Synthetic evidence — combine many trials' effect sizes
C. The causal study already answered "does it work?" The "why/how" question — the levers to scale — is precisely what process evidence addresses by opening the black box between treatment and outcome.
MC2. In this course, "causal evidence" refers specifically to —
  • A. Any study that explains why something happens
  • B. Experimental or quasi-experimental (counterfactual) evidence
  • C. A historian's account of long-term causal processes
  • D. Qualitative interviews about participants' reasons
B. The course reserves "causal" for the counterfactual method — comparing what happened to what would have happened without treatment. Process evidence is a different route to cause and effect (mechanisms over time), not "causal" in this technical sense.
SA3. What are the two "big ideas" of process evidence, and how do they differ?
Context — the environment (legal, historical, social, political, community) in which the intervention happens. Mechanisms — the features of the intervention that actually produce the impacts (or lack of them). Context is the surrounding conditions; mechanisms are the working parts inside. Both are needed to locate the levers of change.
SA4. The Oliveira study is qualitative. How does that shape the way you evaluate its quality?
In two layers. First apply the descriptive-qualitative questions (case logic in sampling, cognitive empathy, describing context) because that's the data type. Then apply the three process questions (identify mechanisms & rule out alternatives; describe how local context affects the process; explore how participants interpret and respond). Judge relevance by authenticity, not generalizability.
SA5. The authors present findings from both teachers' and children's perspectives. Which process quality question does this most directly serve, and why does it strengthen the study?
It most directly serves the third question — how participants interpret and respond to the process. Teachers alone would show Portuguese-as-care but not how children respond; children alone would show resistance but not teachers' motivations. Weaving both builds cognitive empathy and a fuller, more trustworthy account of the two classrooms.
Discussion 1Process evidence and causal evidence answer different questions. Take an intervention you care about and explain what each form could — and could not — tell you about it. Where would relying on only one leave you exposed?
Discussion 2A mechanism is only as good as the alternatives it rules out. For Oliveira et al.'s "children's solidarity work," propose one plausible alternative explanation for the Spanish side-conversations, and say what additional evidence would help you decide between them.

★ Remember this

  • Process evidence answers "how & why," not just "whether." It reveals the causal processes and mechanisms behind an impact — complementing, not replacing, causal evidence.
  • Two big ideas: context and mechanisms. The environment around an intervention, and the working parts inside it that produce the impacts we see.
  • It opens the black box. Causal evidence sees treatment → outcome; process evidence explains the middle — the levers of change that bridge research and practice.
  • Evaluate in two layers: apply the quality questions for the data type(s) used, then the three process questions — identify mechanisms (ruling out alternatives), describe context, and explore how participants interpret the process.
  • Triangulate. How/why questions usually need multiple data types, disciplines, even paradigms — from across the whole evidence framework.
  • Explaining ≠ proving impact. A plausible mechanism isn't proof; process evidence pairs with causal evidence, and implementation fidelity is part of the story.
