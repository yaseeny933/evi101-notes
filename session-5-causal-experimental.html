Causal Experimental Evidence

The evidence that lets us say a program caused a change — not just moved alongside it. Big idea of the day: the counterfactual — what would have happened to the treated group if they hadn't been treated?

01 The core idea — the counterfactual

Causal evidence answers a sharper question than description: "Is a treatment causing an impact?" A treatment is any intervention — a policy, program, or practice. This is the evidence that lets us claim a program actually improved achievement, not merely that it was associated with better outcomes.

To measure the causal impact of a treatment, you compare the outcome for the treated group to a counterfactual: what would have happened to those same people if they had not been treated. The treatment effect is the gap between the two. Causal inference always asks, "compared to what?"

In this week's study, the authors ask whether the Pedagogical Innovation Project (PIP) — which let teachers propose and implement a locally tailored project for a problem of practice in their classrooms — improved language and math scores and grade progression for students in Rio Grande do Norte, Brazil. The real question isn't just "did PIP students do well?" It's "did they do better than they would have under business-as-usual instruction?"

Causal ≠ correlational

Two things can be strongly associated without one causing the other. Descriptive evidence (Session 2) can show that PIP schools and higher scores move together; only causal evidence can license the claim that PIP produced the gain. The whole machinery below exists to rule out the rival explanation that the two groups simply differed to begin with.

02 The fundamental problem — we never observe the counterfactual

Here is the catch that makes causal inference hard: we can never observe the true counterfactual. As soon as a student is taught with PIP, it is impossible to also know what that same student would have scored without it — and vice versa. No individual can be in both conditions at once.

The true individual treatment effect is therefore unknowable. But there's a solution: although we can't know what would have happened to any one person under both conditions, we can estimate the average difference. We build two groups — one that gets the treatment (the treatment group) and one that doesn't (the control group). The control group's outcome stands in for — estimates — the counterfactual for the treatment group.

The control group is our estimate of the counterfactual. If — and only if — the two groups were equivalent before the treatment began, then their difference in outcomes afterward is a good estimate of the average treatment effect. Everything hinges on that "if."

We see the treated group's actual outcome (orange). We never see their counterfactual (dashed) — so we borrow the control group's outcome (teal) to estimate it. The remaining gap is the treatment effect.

03 Random assignment — how we earn a valid counterfactual

Everything reduces to one question: how do we create treatment and control groups that were equivalent before the treatment? The answer is random assignment — the defining feature of a randomized controlled trial (RCT), also just called an experiment.

Note the shift from Session 2. There, we randomly selected a sample from a population. Here, we randomly assign people already in the study to get the treatment or not. In PIP, researchers randomly assigned schools: some got PIP, some got business-as-usual. You could literally flip a coin — heads, you get PIP; tails, you don't.

Because assignment is by chance, there should be no systematic difference between the two groups before the treatment. So if we later see a difference in outcomes, the treatment is the only thing that could have caused it. That is what makes the control group a trustworthy estimate of the treatment group's counterfactual — at least in a reasonably large sample.

Random assignment scatters every kind of school (colors mixed) evenly across both groups. The two groups start balanced, so the only systematic difference left is the treatment itself.

How randomization defeats selection bias

If people choose whether to take a program, the groups can differ systematically from the start — selection bias. Maybe only the most motivated teachers sign up for PIP; then any score gain might reflect motivation, not the program. You'd get a biased (wrong) answer about the impact.

Randomization removes choice from the equation. Every school has an equal chance of landing in either group, so motivation — and every other characteristic — is spread evenly across treatment and control. That lets us rule out the rival explanation that the groups just differed to begin with. This is why RCTs are said to have high internal validity: they close off alternative explanations for the result.

Random assignment RCT

A coin flip decides who gets the treatment. Groups are balanced by chance, so the treatment is the only systematic difference.

Result: a valid counterfactual and high internal validity — a difference in outcomes can be attributed to the treatment.

Self-selection biased

People opt in or out. The groups may differ in motivation, resources, or ability before anything happens.

Result: selection bias — you can't tell whether the treatment or the pre-existing difference produced the outcome.

↳ One honest caveat from the video: randomization is not a guarantee for any single trial. Chance can still leave small differences between groups — randomization minimizes the possibility of systematic differences, and does so most reliably in reasonably large samples.

04 Reading & judging an RCT

When you meet a causal experimental study, you judge it on its own terms. The effect is reported as an effect size, and three essential questions from the learning outcomes tell you whether to trust and how far to extend the finding.

Effect size — the difference, in standard-deviation units

In causal evidence, the effect size is the difference in outcomes between the treatment and control groups, measured in standard-deviation (SD) units. Reporting in SDs (rather than raw points) makes effects comparable across different tests and scales — an effect of "0.2 SD" means the average treated student moved two-tenths of a standard deviation above the average control student. As a rough field convention, education effects around 0.1 SD are small, ~0.2–0.3 SD are meaningful, and larger ones are notable — but always weigh practical significance, not just the number.

How well does the study describe the contrast between the treatment and the control group?

The contrast IS the treatment The effect only means something relative to what the control group experienced. PIP looks smaller where control schools also gave teachers autonomy, and larger where controls got only rote instruction. A study should describe both conditions — a weakness of this paper is that it explains the treatment more fully than the control.

How valid is the estimate of the counterfactual? (How well does the study deal with selection bias?)

Internal validity Are we convinced treated and control students would have had the same outcomes but for the treatment? Random assignment is what buys this — it lets you dismiss "the PIP schools were just more motivated" as unlikely, because motivation was balanced across groups by chance.

How well do the authors identify the groups to which the treatment effect applies?

Who does it apply to? In an RCT the effect is usually the average effect on the whole sample — but the average may hide variation. PIP's gains were concentrated in Grade 6, were more pronounced for students already scoring highly, and differed somewhat by gender. Don't over-extrapolate from an average to every subgroup.

What an RCT can and can't tell you

A clean RCT supports strong claims about whether a treatment worked — but it can't tell you how or why it worked, what it did to outcomes that weren't measured, or what the effect would have been against a different control condition. Causal experimental evidence is one tool among many; the course's process evidence (later) explores the "how and why."

⚠ Nuance & watch-outs

  • Correlation ≠ causation. An association between PIP and higher scores is not proof PIP caused them. Only a valid counterfactual — here, from random assignment — licenses the causal claim.
  • Randomization balances the unobserved too. Its real power: it evens out not just measured traits (baseline scores) but characteristics you never thought to measure (motivation, home support). No statistical control can do that.
  • Internal validity ≠ generalizability. A study can be internally airtight yet not transfer. RCT participants are often unusual — the sickest patients, or schools near research universities. These PIP schools had better baseline outcomes, so impacts may not extend to weaker schools or to São Paulo.
  • Statistical vs. practical significance. A statistically significant effect can still be tiny in SD terms; a large effect size in a small study can be noisy. Ask both "is it real?" and "is it big enough to matter?"
  • The average hides subgroups. An average treatment effect can be driven by one grade or one type of student. Check whom the effect actually applies to before acting on it.
  • Ethics & paradigm. RCTs sit in a post-positivist paradigm (an objective impact exists to be estimated). Randomizing a potentially harmful treatment (e.g., grade retention) is ethically fraught, and over-researching marginalized communities carries real costs.

Policymakers have decided to implement PIP in São Paulo schools, but some worry the evidence shows PIP's impact is concentrated in Grade 6 learning. As always, there is no single correct answer — the task is to use the language of evidence to argue for one choice over another.

How to argue it in the language of evidence: Ground the choice in Session 5 vocabulary. Then, on the G6 concern, be explicit about whom the effect applies to: an average masks subgroups, so either argue the progression/retention effects generalize beyond G6, or concede the effect is grade-specific and justify implementing anyway. Close by flagging generalizability from Rio Grande do Norte to São Paulo.

06 Glossary — quiz yourself first

Try to define each term before you reveal it. These are the Session 5 evidence-vocabulary words.

causal impact
The change in an outcome that a treatment actually produced — as opposed to a mere association. Answering "is a treatment causing an impact?" requires comparing the treated outcome to a counterfactual. "PIP raised math scores by X" is a causal-impact claim; "PIP schools had higher scores" is not.
counterfactual
What would have happened to the treatment group if they had not received the treatment. It is never directly observed, so it must be estimated — usually with a control/comparison group. The counterfactual is the central idea of causal inference. For PIP students, the counterfactual is their scores under business-as-usual instruction.
treatment
The intervention whose impact is being measured — a policy, program, or practice. In this study, the treatment is being in a school that implemented PIP. "Treatment" doesn't mean medicine here — it's any deliberate intervention.
treatment group
The participants who receive the treatment. Their post-intervention outcome is compared against the control group to estimate the effect. The schools randomly assigned to run PIP.
control group
The participants who do not receive the treatment (they get business-as-usual). The control group's outcome is used to estimate the counterfactual for the treatment group. A well-chosen control group answers "compared to what?"
random assignment
Assigning participants to the treatment or control group purely by chance (like a coin flip). It makes the groups equivalent before treatment, so the treatment becomes the only systematic difference between them. Distinct from random sampling, which selects who's in the study. In PIP, schools were randomly assigned to receive the program or not.
randomized controlled trial (RCT)
An experiment that uses random assignment to create treatment and control groups, then compares their outcomes to estimate a causal effect. The gold-standard design for internal validity. Also just called "an experiment."
selection bias
Systematic distortion that arises when people are not randomly assigned — e.g., they choose whether to participate — so treatment and control groups differ before the treatment. It produces a biased (incorrect) estimate of impact. Random assignment is what prevents it. If only the most motivated teachers adopt PIP, motivation — not PIP — could explain the gains.
average treatment effect
The difference between the average outcome of the treatment group and the average outcome of the control group. It's an average — it may not equal the effect for any particular subgroup within the sample. PIP's average effect masked the fact that gains were concentrated in Grade 6.
effect size
The magnitude of the treatment effect, expressed in standard-deviation units so it's comparable across different scales and tests. Bigger = a larger causal impact — but judge practical significance, not just the number. A 0.2-SD effect ≈ the average treated student outscoring two-thirds of the control group's typical spread.
internal validity
How confident we are that the treatment — and not some other difference between the groups — caused the observed outcome; equivalently, how valid the estimate of the counterfactual is. RCTs have high internal validity because randomization rules out alternative explanations. "Would the treated and control students have had the same outcomes but for the treatment?"
external validity (generalizability)
The extent to which a study's findings extend to other people, places, or settings beyond the one studied. High internal validity does not guarantee it — RCT participants are often unusual, so transfer must be argued, not assumed. Would PIP's Rio Grande do Norte results hold in São Paulo? That's an external-validity question.

07 iRAT prep — test yourself

Answer before revealing. Three are written like TBL readiness-check multiple-choice; the rest are short-answer/application. Then chew on the discussion prompts.

MC1. Why can we never directly measure the true counterfactual for a treated student?
  • A. Because samples are always too small
  • B. Because the same student can't be both treated and untreated at once
  • C. Because standard deviations are hard to compute
  • D. Because control groups are unethical
B. As soon as a student experiences PIP, their "what would have happened without PIP" outcome is unobservable — and vice versa. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference, which is why we estimate the counterfactual with a control group.
MC2. A district lets schools volunteer for a new tutoring program, then compares volunteers to non-volunteers. The biggest threat to a causal claim is —
  • A. The effect size wasn't reported in SD units
  • B. The sample wasn't drawn from the whole population
  • C. Selection bias — volunteering schools may differ systematically from non-volunteers before any tutoring happens
  • D. There is no control group at all
C. Because schools chose their own group, the groups may differ in motivation, resources, or leadership from the start. Without random assignment, we can't tell whether tutoring or those pre-existing differences produced the outcome. (There is a comparison group — the problem is how it was formed.)
MC3. An RCT reports an average treatment effect of 0.25 SD on math scores. Which interpretation is best supported?
  • A. Every student in the treatment group gained 0.25 SD
  • B. On average, the treatment group scored about a quarter of a standard deviation above the control group
  • C. The treatment raised scores by 25 percentage points
  • D. The result is automatically practically important
B. Effect size is a difference in averages, in SD units — not a per-student guarantee (A), not percentage points (C). And a statistically detectable 0.25 SD still needs a separate judgment about whether it's practically meaningful (D).
SA4. In one sentence, what does random assignment accomplish that random sampling does not?
Random assignment makes the treatment and control groups equivalent before the treatment (balancing observed and unobserved traits), so a later difference in outcomes can be attributed to the treatment — it creates a valid counterfactual and internal validity. Random sampling only makes the study group representative of a population (external/descriptive inference); it says nothing about causation.
SA5. PIP was a clean RCT with high internal validity. Name two things it still cannot tell São Paulo's leaders.
Any two of: (1) how or why PIP worked — the mechanism/process; (2) its effect on outcomes that weren't measured; (3) what the effect would have been against a different control condition; (4) whether the result generalizes to São Paulo, since these Rio Grande do Norte schools had better-than-average baseline outcomes; (5) the effect for a specific subgroup, since the reported effect is an average (e.g., gains concentrated in Grade 6).
Discussion 1A colleague says, "The PIP schools scored higher, so PIP works — let's roll it out everywhere." Using the vocabulary of causal evidence (counterfactual, random assignment, internal vs. external validity, average treatment effect), lay out what the RCT does justify and what it does not — especially given that impacts were concentrated in Grade 6.
Discussion 2Some interventions can't ethically be tested with an RCT — e.g., randomly holding students back a grade. Pick an education policy you care about, decide whether an RCT would be ethical and feasible, and if not, describe what kind of counterfactual you might construct instead.
