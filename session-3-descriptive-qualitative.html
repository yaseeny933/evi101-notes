Descriptive Qualitative Evidence

Same question as the numbers — "What is happening?" — answered in words. The big idea: for depth, choose cases that illustrate the phenomenon.

case logic purposeful sampling palpability · heterogeneity the Calarco study

01 The core idea

Descriptive qualitative evidence answers the same question as descriptive quantitative evidence — "What is happening?" — but it describes "what is" in words rather than numbers, and it takes a very different route to get there.

The goal of qualitative research is not to produce findings that generalize to a whole population. Its goal is depth: a detailed explanation of a particular phenomenon in a specific setting. Quantitative descriptive evidence gives you breadth (what's happening across a city, state, or nation); qualitative gives you depth (what's happening, richly, in one place).

The OECD/PISA report and the Calarco study both illuminate socioeconomic inequality — one by breadth, one by depth. Complementary, not competing.

Qualitative approaches are especially well suited to understanding people's beliefs, experiences, and motivations. Researchers work mostly with text-based data — interviews, observations, focus groups, documents — and code it (tag it with meaningful category labels), then group codes into themes: recurring patterns and significant ideas that reveal the deeper story in participants' words and actions. As Denzin and Lincoln put it, qualitative researchers "study things in their natural settings, attempting to make sense of… phenomena in terms of the meanings people bring to them."

02 Case logic vs. sampling logic

This is the key contrast with Session 2. Both approaches must choose who to study — but they choose in opposite ways, for opposite reasons.

Left: the OECD/PISA logic — a representative random sample stands in for everyone. Right: the Calarco logic — a few cases, chosen deliberately, reveal a phenomenon richly.

Exposure, not size. Imagine Calarco had visited 50 classrooms for one day each. She would have more cases but shallower data. By spending two years in one school, she gained the depth, trust, and rapport (cognitive empathy) that let her see how context shapes behavior. In case logic, more time in fewer places usually beats more places for less time.

03 What high-quality qualitative evidence looks like

Because quantitative norms (generalizability, randomization) came to dominate social science, people often misapplied those criteria to qualitative work and judged it "not rigorous." That's a paradigm error. Qualitative evidence should be judged on its own terms. Calarco & Small name five quality factors — cognitive empathy, heterogeneity, palpability, follow-up, self-awareness. A strong study needn't have all five. This session focuses on three:

PalpabilityConcrete, not abstract. Participants' own words — quotes and rich descriptions — so we see the world through their eyes, not just the researcher's summary.
HeterogeneityPeople shown as full, complex, sometimes contradictory humans — not stereotyped or oversimplified. Shows variation and exceptions within a group.
Follow-upDid researchers adapt over time — track how the context changed, chase new questions that arose during the study itself?

These map onto the three essential quality questions for evaluating any descriptive qualitative study:

How well does the study select participants or cases purposefully to address the research question?

case logic The cases chosen should give a real chance to examine the context, experience, or process the question is about. Representativeness is not the test; fit-to-question and exposure are.

How well does the study illustrate participants' perspectives?

palpability Look for quotes and paraphrases in participants' own words — concrete scenes, not abstract claims.

How well does the study describe the context of participants' experiences?

heterogeneity + follow-up Rich context signals strong exposure; it lets us see people as diverse and complex and see how the setting changed over time.

Palpability in Calarco — Anna & Ms. Dunham (p. 869)

"Anna looks up at Ms. Dunham and calls out loudly: 'Ms. Dunham, I need help! Ms. Dunham!' … Ms. Dunham nods, noting distractedly: 'I'll be there soon.' Anna, however, does not give up, adding: 'I can't get it to stay so I can do 170 degrees!'" Calarco (2011), p. 869 — hearing it in their own voices makes the finding palpable

Heterogeneity in Calarco — Jesse (p. 870)

Called on to read, Jesse "frantically scans the page, his eyes growing big and fearful." Ms. Dunham quietly feeds him the line ("Mercy went… page 206"); he answers "oh, OK" and reads on. Yet Jesse "was usually outgoing and eager to participate… but he rarely asked for help." Calarco (2011), p. 870 — one scene shows contradiction within a single child; strip the context and you lose his full humanity

Context detail — "while the brown brick building is old, Maplewood's classrooms are clean, colorful, and brightly lit" — signals the deep exposure that makes heterogeneity possible. On follow-up, we see little in "I need help!"; the informed guess is that two years in the school revealed no major contextual changes — and a study needn't show all five factors to be high quality.

⚑ Nuance & watch-outs

  • Qualitative ≠ "just anecdotes." Systematic coding, iterative rounds of data collection, and thematic saturation make this rigorous evidence — not a pile of stories.
  • A small, non-random sample is a feature here, not a flaw. The goal is depth, not generalization, so "n is tiny" and "it's not representative" are the wrong complaints for case-logic work.
  • Don't judge qualitative work by quantitative criteria. Asking whether Calarco's findings "generalize to all schools" makes as little sense as asking whether PISA can tell you about one classroom in São Paulo. Match the criteria to the paradigm.
  • Palpability is not cherry-picking. Good quotes illustrate patterns that emerged from saturated, coded data. Hand-picking a few vivid lines to prove a point you already decided on is the opposite of quality.
  • Depth is a trade-off, not a free lunch. One deeply-studied school buys rich understanding but cannot, by itself, tell you how common the pattern is. That's what quantitative breadth is for — the two are complementary.

04 The Calarco study — "I need help!"

Jessica Calarco spent two years observing and interviewing children and teachers at Maplewood Elementary, studying how students of different social-class backgrounds seek help in class. Her key finding: children play an active role in shaping their own classroom experiences — they are not passive recipients of what teachers hand out.

The cultural-capital mechanism. Teachers and rules gave little explicit guidance about when and how to seek help. Into that vacuum, students drew on their class backgrounds: middle-class children more readily and assertively requested help (calling out, persisting, negotiating), while working-class children tended to work through problems on their own. Those help-seeking strategies only become cultural capital — yielding real advantages — when they align with what the institution rewards. Same behaviors, different payoff depending on fit with the setting.

The study is a model of case logic: one purposefully-chosen site, deep exposure, participants' own voices (Anna, Jesse, Ms. Dunham), and careful context — all in service of explaining a phenomenon in depth, not estimating a population.

Data example A — Anna (p. 869) calls out loudly and repeatedly until Ms. Dunham comes. The assertive strategy "pays off" only because the classroom tolerates and rewards calling out — evidence that the institution's response, not the behavior alone, converts help-seeking into advantage.
Data example B — Jesse (p. 870) rarely asks for help, yet gets quietly rescued by Ms. Dunham feeding him the line. His need is met only because the teacher happened to notice — showing that when strategies don't align with explicit norms, whether they "profit" is left to teacher discretion. If teachers deliberately recognized quieter help-seeking, the payoff would be less arbitrary.

§ Glossary — quiz yourself first

Try to define each term before you reveal it.

case logic
Selecting cases intentionally / purposefully because they illustrate a theory, context, or phenomenon of interest — not to represent a broader population. The organizing logic of qualitative sampling and the big idea of the day. Calarco chose one school and stayed two years to illustrate class-based help-seeking in depth.
purposeful sampling
Choosing participants or cases on purpose because they give a good opportunity to examine the context, experience, or process central to the research question — as opposed to random or representative sampling. The first essential quality question asks exactly this: were cases selected purposefully to address the research question?
exposure
The amount of time a researcher spends with participants. In case logic, exposure matters more than sample size — it builds trust and rapport (cognitive empathy) and deepens understanding of how context shapes behavior. Two years in one school = high exposure and deep data; one day in 50 schools = low exposure and shallow data.
palpability
Whether the evidence is concrete rather than abstract — how well the study illustrates participants' perspectives and behaviors through quotes or rich descriptions, ideally in their own words, so we see the world through their eyes. Anna's "Ms. Dunham, I need help!" makes the finding palpable.
heterogeneity
Portraying participants and contexts as diverse, full, and complex — not stereotyped or oversimplified. It can show contradiction within one person, variation within a group, or exceptions. Rich context and strong exposure make it possible, and it guards against inaccurate generalizations. Jesse is outgoing yet freezes and rarely asks for help — complexity within a single child.
thematic saturation
The point in iterative rounds of data collection and coding where researchers keep seeing similar patterns across cases and gathering or analyzing more data reveals no new insights. A signal that the themes are well-grounded. You stop collecting when new classrooms stop teaching you anything new.
follow-up
How well researchers adapted over time — tracking how the research context changed, or collecting new data to answer questions that arose during the study itself. One of Calarco & Small's five quality factors; part of describing context. "I need help!" shows little follow-up — plausibly because two years revealed no major changes in the setting.

★ Remember this

  • Same question, different route. Qualitative descriptive evidence answers "What is happening?" in words, aiming for depth in one setting — not generalization to a population.
  • Case logic, not sampling logic. Cases are chosen purposefully to illustrate a phenomenon, not drawn randomly to represent the many.
  • Exposure over size. Time with participants beats number of participants — it buys depth, trust, and cognitive empathy.
  • Quality = palpability + heterogeneity + context (plus cognitive empathy, follow-up, self-awareness). Judge it on qualitative terms, never by quantitative criteria.
  • Calarco's finding: children actively shape their classroom lives; class-based help-seeking becomes cultural capital only when it aligns with what the institution rewards.
  • Not just anecdotes. Systematic coding to thematic saturation makes small, non-random qualitative work rigorous — depth is the point.
