01 The core idea — the Evidence Framework

The big idea for the whole course, and this session: each form of evidence has a unique affordance. Each type answers a different question and lets us make a different type of inference. The central skill is deciding whether you are using the right type of evidence for the inference you want to make.

An affordance is what a given type of evidence lets you do — the kind of question it can answer and the kind of conclusion it can support. Descriptive evidence can tell you what is happening; it cannot, on its own, tell you what caused it. Match the evidence to the claim, or the inference breaks.

The four types of evidence

You'll draw on all four to build your final recommendation to São Paulo. A single study can contain several types at once (e.g., Calarco's classroom study is mostly descriptive-qualitative but also reports descriptive-quantitative counts of help requests).

TypeQuestion it answersTypical methods
DescriptiveWhat is happening? (e.g. what is happening with income inequality in Brazil?)Quantitative (numbers describing a population) or qualitative (words describing a local setting)
CausalIs a policy, program, or practice causing an impact?Experiments and quasi-experiments
SyntheticWhat do we already know about this topic?Synthesizes / combines prior research
ProcessNot what is happening, but how and why?Studies of mechanisms and implementation

The three throughlines of the whole course

Every session returns to these three ideas. They are the lens you use on every reading.

Inference the goal

A conclusion drawn on the basis of reasoning and evidence. The course is one long exercise in making sound inferences — and noticing when a claim outruns its evidence.

Ask: what conclusion does this evidence actually license?

Quality how strong

How well the claims are supported by the type of evidence used. Each type is judged on its own terms — but two questions apply to all: how well-supported are the claims, and what are the limitations?

Ask: what claims can we not make from this?

Relevance applies to me?

Whether high-quality findings actually apply to your context. Evidence gathered "there and then" must be reasoned into "here and now" for São Paulo's challenge.

Ask: how similar are the setting & sample — and would the differences matter?

02 The Grand Challenge

The course is organized around one real problem — the Grand Challenge — and it is also your final, culminating assignment. In the spirit of a "no surprises" policy, you see the final before class even begins.

The Grand Challenge: How can we improve academic achievement in the foundational subjects of math, reading, and science for students in years 6–9 in the São Paulo school system? Underneath it sits the field's larger question: how do we improve learning and advance equity in pursuit of a more just society?

The Brazilian context (from the OECD reading)

Brazil's education system has made great progress over the last couple of decades — the vast majority of children are now enrolled in basic education. But there are great disparities in access and achievement along class lines, and these deepened during the COVID-19 pandemic. Much of the reading is descriptive quantitative evidence about income inequality.

You will use evidence in four ways

Your final recommendation to the São Paulo Secretary of Education has four moves — and each pulls on the evidence framework:

Frame the issue — highlight the dimension of the problem people should focus on.

Framing Descriptive evidence helps you decide what the problem really is before proposing fixes.

Discuss considerations for expanding a curricular innovation to São Paulo.

Relevance Was the innovation shown to work elsewhere? Would it transfer "here and now"?

Recommend a form of teacher coaching to support that expansion.

Causal + process What coaching causes improvement, and how/why does it work?

Recommend additional evidence São Paulo should collect to keep improving.

Affordances Which type of evidence fills the gaps that current evidence can't?

03 The Ladder of Inference & confirmation bias

One way to picture inference is as a ladder. It breaks the move from raw data to action into steps — and shows where we tend to slip. The farther you climb from the data, the more careful you must be that your conclusion is actually warranted.

Data → select → interpret → conclude → act. The higher the rung, the more the conclusion depends on the type of evidence being adequate.

The data at the bottom aren't only research findings — they also include your professional expertise, personal relationships, and knowledge of local context, and can be quantitative (numbers) or qualitative (words, stories). Our brains are wired to make meaning out of data — that's how we make sense of the world — but it makes it dangerously easy to jump up the ladder, adding interpretation and conclusions without supporting data.

Worked example — the OECD report (p. 6)

The authors model the ladder in a single passage:

CONCLUSION FIRST → "Brazil is marked by deep-rooted inequalities by race and class." (How do we know we can trust this?)

SELECTED DATA → In 2018, Black and mixed Brazilians were more than half the population but over 75% of the poorest income decile.

INTERPRETATION → Not just overrepresentation — little indication the race-based gap will narrow (Figure 1.3).

CONCLUSION / ACTION → Inequality is deep-rooted and intersectional — grounds for specific education interventions in your Grand Challenge.

Nuance & watch-outs

  • Don't jump the ladder. It's easy to add interpretation and draw conclusions without the supporting data. Start on the lowest rung: name your observation before stating your interpretation, conclusion, or action.
  • Confirmation bias runs in one direction. We readily spot where an author "jumped the ladder" when we disagree with the conclusion — but wave through unjustified inferences when we agree. Staying low on the ladder forces you to clarify the data you're actually relying on, and works against this very human error.
  • "There and then" ≠ "here and now." High quality is not enough — evidence from another setting still has to be reasoned into São Paulo's context. Non-São-Paulo data isn't irrelevant; it just takes more work to see how it applies.
  • No evidence type is "best." Affordances differ. Judge each type on its own terms — and check you're using the right type for the inference you want.

04 Paradigms — the worldview behind the evidence

Who decides what counts as good evidence? That depends on the researcher's paradigm — the worldview that structures their approach to knowledge, their research goals, and how they judge quality. Two researchers in different paradigms can look at the same data and genuinely see different things.

Think of the duck–rabbit picture: the same image reads as a duck or a rabbit depending on the lens you bring. Likewise, a paradigm sets the conventions for what data to collect, how to analyze it, what counts as convincing, and which inferences you can draw. Know a researcher's paradigm before you judge their work — so you apply the conventions they were working under.

This course adapts the classification of Lincoln, Lynham & Guba into five common paradigms. You don't need to memorize the details — the point is to feel the range. Two distinctions separate them: their views on whether/how objectivity is possible, and on the researcher's influence on the research.

Positivismstable truth · discover itA single objective reality exists and can be uncovered through careful measurement. Patterns are independent of the researcher's values. Common in the hard sciences and experimental psychology.
Post-positivismtruth, but only partialThere is an objective truth, but we capture only facets of it — with error — by triangulating multiple data sources. Most course readings, and many quantitative studies, live here.
Interpretivismhow do people interpret it?Cares about the worldviews of those studied — and how the researcher's own values shape their reading of them. Asks not "what is happening?" but "how do people interpret what's happening?" Common in qualitative evidence.
Participatoryknowledge co-constructedResearchers and participants make sense of the world together. Questions whether an "outsider" should try to interpret others' worldviews at all — knowledge is a shared project.
Criticalwho has power & voice?Unearths the social, political, and economic forces shaping who speaks and who is heard. Overlaps with participatory, but adds explicit critique of power structures — and often argues for redistributing power.

Key points about paradigms

  • No paradigm is more objective, valid, or biased than another. They differ on whether/how objectivity is possible — and different biases surface in different ones.
  • Paradigms are loose frameworks, not fixed boxes. A researcher can hold several at once, and multiple can appear in one piece of research.
  • We need multiple paradigms. Fully addressing educational challenges takes evidence from many perspectives — a strength of education research.
  • Where does Evidence sit? The course takes a post-positivist stance — not because it's "best," but because it's dominant in education policy and practice, so it prepares you to work within it beyond HGSE. (That dominance is historical — e.g., Cold-War-era economic thinking — and has meant qualitative, participatory, and critical traditions often held less power in policy settings.)

Q1 · MCThe "unique affordance" of a type of evidence refers to…
  1. How much it costs to collect.
  2. The kind of question it can answer and the type of inference it lets you make.
  3. Whether it is quantitative or qualitative.
  4. How recent the study is.
Answer: B. An affordance is what a type of evidence lets you do — the question it answers and the inference it supports. Descriptive evidence answers "what is happening?"; causal evidence answers "is this causing an impact?" The core skill is matching evidence type to the inference you want. Watch-out: A and D are about logistics/recency, not affordance; C is a real distinction but only within descriptive evidence.
Q2 · MCA researcher assumes there is an objective truth but that we can only capture facets of it, with error, by triangulating multiple data sources. Which paradigm?
  1. Positivism
  2. Interpretivism
  3. Post-positivism
  4. Critical
Answer: C — Post-positivism. Like positivism it assumes an objective truth, but unlike positivism it holds we grasp only partial facets, with error, via triangulation. This is also the paradigm the Evidence course itself adopts — because it dominates education policy and practice, not because it is "best." Positivism (A) claims truth is fully discoverable; interpretivism (B) asks how people interpret events; critical (D) focuses on power.
Q3 · Short answerList the rungs of the ladder of inference in order, and state the one rule the video gives for climbing it well.
Bottom to top: (1) data (everything your senses take in) → (2) select data → (3) interpret (add meaning) → (4) draw a conclusion → (5) take action. The rule: start on the lowest rung — name your observation before stating your interpretation, conclusion, or next action — which keeps inferences grounded in the data and guards against jumping the ladder. The farther you get from the data, the more careful you must be that the conclusion suits the type of evidence available.
Q4 · ApplicationYou strongly agree with a study's conclusion, so you accept it quickly without scrutinizing its evidence. Name the reasoning error and the habit that counters it.
This is confirmation bias — the human tendency to seek and accept information confirming our pre-existing beliefs while minimizing evidence for other explanations. It's asymmetric: we readily catch "ladder-jumping" in arguments we disagree with, but wave it through when we agree. The counter-habit is staying low on the ladder of inference: force yourself to name the specific data supporting the claim before endorsing the conclusion.
Q5 · ApplicationA well-designed U.S. study shows a math program raised achievement. Before recommending it for São Paulo, which throughline concept must you apply, and what two questions does it ask?
Relevance — whether findings gathered "there and then" apply "here and now." Ask: (1) What inferences from the study would inform or apply to São Paulo's Grand Challenge? and (2) How related are the study's setting and sample to São Paulo's context, priorities, and needs — and how or why would any differences matter? High quality alone isn't enough; a strong finding from elsewhere still has to be reasoned into São Paulo's context. Non-São-Paulo evidence isn't irrelevant — it just takes more work to establish how it applies.

§ Glossary — quiz yourself first

Try to define each term before you reveal it. These are the Session 1 vocabulary plus key terms from the videos.

inference
A conclusion drawn on the basis of reasoning and evidence. Making sound inferences — and spotting when a claim outruns its evidence — is the central activity of the whole course. "Brazil has deep-rooted inequality" is an inference; the income-decile figures are the data behind it.
affordance
What a given type of evidence lets you do — the question it can answer and the kind of inference it can support. Each of the four evidence types has a different one, so no single type is universally "best." Descriptive evidence affords "what is happening?"; causal evidence affords "is X causing an impact?"
quality
How well a study's claims are supported by the type of evidence it uses. Each evidence type is evaluated on its own terms, but two questions always apply: How well-supported are the claims? and What are the limitations — what claims can't we make? Higher quality → stronger inferences. Knowing the limits → fewer inappropriate inferences.
relevance
Whether high-quality findings actually apply to your context — how evidence collected "there and then" transfers "here and now." Ask what inferences would apply to São Paulo, and how similar the setting and sample are (and whether the differences matter). A finding needn't be about São Paulo to be relevant — it just takes more reasoning to apply it.
ladder of inference
A model that breaks inference into steps — data → select data → interpret → conclude → act — to show where reasoning can go wrong. Staying low on the ladder (naming observations before interpretations) keeps conclusions grounded in the data. "Jumping the ladder" = drawing conclusions without the supporting data.
confirmation bias
The human tendency to seek out and accept information that confirms our pre-existing beliefs while minimizing or ignoring evidence for other explanations. It's asymmetric — we scrutinize arguments we disagree with far more than ones we agree with. Counter it by staying low on the ladder: name the data before endorsing the conclusion.
the four evidence types
Descriptive (what is happening? — quantitative or qualitative), causal (is X causing an impact? — experiments & quasi-experiments), synthetic (what do we already know? — synthesizes prior research), and process (how and why?). One study can contain several types at once.
paradigm
The worldview that structures a researcher's approach to knowledge — their research goals, conventions for collecting/analyzing data, and standards for what counts as good research. Two researchers in different paradigms can see different things in the same data (the duck–rabbit). Five discussed: positivism, post-positivism, interpretivism, participatory, critical.
post-positivism
The paradigm assuming there is an objective truth, but that we capture only facets of it — with error — by triangulating multiple data sources. The dominant paradigm in education policy/practice, and the stance the Evidence course adopts (for that reason, not because it's "best"). Contrast positivism, which holds the full truth is discoverable if methods are careful enough.
triangulation
Using multiple sources or types of data to converge on a finding, since any single source captures only part of the truth (with error). A hallmark move of the post-positivist paradigm.

★ Remember this

  • Each form of evidence has a unique affordance — it answers a different question and licenses a different inference. Use the right type for the claim you want to make.
  • Four types: descriptive (what?), causal (is X causing it?), synthetic (what do we know?), process (how & why?). A single study can mix them.
  • Three throughlines run the whole course: inference (draw sound conclusions), quality (how well evidence supports its claims, judged on its own terms), and relevance ("there and then" → "here and now").
  • Climb the ladder carefully. Data → select → interpret → conclude → act. Name the observation before the interpretation, and you'll dodge confirmation bias.
  • Paradigms are lenses, not rankings. No paradigm is more valid or biased; the course works in post-positivism because it dominates policy — while treating all paradigms as equally valid.
